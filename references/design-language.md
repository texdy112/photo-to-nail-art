# Design language and aesthetic direction (internal reference)

These notes refine creative decisions; they are not a rigid fill-in-the-blanks template and must not appear as a user-facing prompt instead of an actual image.

## A. Read the uploaded image and the broader visual context

Determine what the input *is*: unmistakable character/IP, independent illustration, fashion photo, landscape, food, graphic texture, or object. Recognizable characters: respect characteristic eyes, ears, muzzle, facial spacing, costume and accessories. Separate directly visible properties from uncertain off-image franchise claims.

When known and useful, extend toward a *visual world*: iconic props, patterns, story-associated objects or era aesthetics. Keep distinct three sources: **IP-core**, **plausibly associated**, **designer-added stylistic accents**. A colorful electronic toy, for example, is not automatically an official object within every cartoon franchise. Do not falsely claim IP provenance.

## B. Build a collection identity BEFORE choosing tips

Silently consider several concepts with genuinely different visual approaches, then pick the most coherent. Choose a dominant color family, complementary secondary colors and a rare accent; adjust saturation and relative areas to achieve better harmony than mechanical sampling. Concepts may be playful, retro, calm or graphic as the original suggests. Do not default everything to bubblegum-pink or generic Y2K.

The chosen concept should determine what is *excluded*. Avoid multiple competing themes in a single set.

## C. Four useful levels of translation

1. **Figurative recognition:** a small re-drawn character or iconic symbolic prop, designed for an elongated nail surface.
2. **Local deconstruction:** one eye pair, ear, paw, ribbon, screen button, collar shape, silhouette, flower petal, face expression or costume piece.
3. **Graphic abstraction:** a useful curve becomes a French edge; round cheeks inspire ordered circles; clothing grids inspire real check patterns; a landscape boundary becomes a clean color-block; an accessory can be reduced to a line-art symbol.
4. **Creative recomposition:** combine fragments in a coherent composition that was not a direct crop of the original.

Do not force abstract patterns onto an input whose theme is better expressed by bold negative space or one beautifully painted subject.

## D. Structure with intent, not quotas

A successful 10-tip set often contains several roles:
- **Hero:** one or more recognizable, exceptionally well painted motifs.
- **Supporting theme:** symbols or props connected to the subject.
- **Pattern:** only if appropriate; polka dots, gingham, stripes, scallops, swirls or another justified repeating system.
- **Material:** restrained syrup gel, cat-eye, pearl, glitter, semi-transparent tint or raised gel when it helps the concept.
- **Balance:** a quiet but composed tip, e.g. asymmetric French arc, purposeful solid block or controlled gradient.

Roles can overlap. Counts vary by source. Alternate busy, medium and quiet tips; avoid putting all heroes together. If the hero tips are covered, the other tips should still look artistically designed.

## E. Placement is a major part of design

Consider the physical canvas: tapered nail silhouette, small available area, cuticle edge and curved tip. Use at least occasional intentional asymmetry:
- A half face peeking from one side rather than another centered sticker portrait.
- A single eye/ear detail emerging from the cuticle edge.
- Partial graphic cropping that looks chosen, never like a failed photo crop.
- A tiny meaningful symbol that makes the viewer look closer.
- Different motif sizes; negative space used confidently.

The result should work from a distance as a color/shape composition and reward close inspection with small, meaningful design details.

## F. Craft realism

2D character drawing with smooth outlines and consistent color blocks often yields the most faithful and elegant outcome. Distinguish a real gel-painted cartoon from an image transfer: visible craft decisions, adapted placement, reduced detail. If a 3D accent is helpful, scale it to a real nail and avoid thick raised characters across multiple tips. Complex creativity can result from composition, palette, matte/gloss contrast, restrained materials and precise freehand painting—not a high embellishment count.

## G. Pattern discipline

- **Polka dots:** recognizably ordered group, consistent/rhythmically varied scale.
- **Gingham/checks:** intentional grid and measured color/line widths.
- **Stripes:** consistent direction or clearly art-directed changes of direction.
- **Stars, bows, clouds:** use only when narratively or graphically relevant.
- **Small motif groups:** each group should read as a recognizable symbol/expressive mini-composition, never randomly scattered speckles.

**Negative tests:** miniature poster? arbitrary decoration filler? every nail equally busy? 3D charms making the object look like a toy rather than a manicure? If yes, revise the plan before generating.

## H. Inspiration vs copying

User-provided premium nail references are guidance on motif distribution, negative space, placement, craft and pattern rhythm, **not** permission to reproduce the entire existing set. Use their design grammar to create a fresh collection tied to the user's supplied subject. Do not bundle reference photos, copyrighted screenshots, personal uploads or private testing briefs in the public skill package.
